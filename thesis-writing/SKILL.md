## Assumption

This skill assumes you've chosen thesis-mode. If the document needs to be a proposal (seeking approval), a mandate (decision already made), or a review (sharpening someone else's work), stop and pick a different approach. A thesis is a claim offered for scrutiny — the reader could overrule you and that should be fine.

---

## Eight principles

### 1. Thesis ≠ mandate
If the document reads as a decision, readers either comply silently or resist the framing. Both are worse than dialogue. Watch for "must," "will," "all teams are required to." Replace with "I think," "my read is," "this would require."

### 2. Deterministic language projects false certainty
Every paragraph closing with "this will achieve X" overstates. Use "this suggests," "this points toward," "this is what I'd bet on."

### 3. Observations > proposals > non-negotiables
Build in this order:
- **Observations** — datable, named, unfalsifiable, inviting
- **Proposals** — what you think it means; debatable
- **Non-negotiables** — what must be true regardless; cap at 3

Non-negotiables are the hard edges of reader autonomy — small, explicit, load-bearing. If they outnumber proposals, it's a mandate, not a thesis.

### 4. Separate thinking from doing
Thesis document = the thinking. Execution detail (timelines, owners, rollout) = separate appendix. Mixing makes the thinking harder to refine and the execution harder to own.

### 5. Open questions as a feature
If every question is answered, it's a proposal. Minimum one "I don't know" or "I'd like your take on X" per major section.

### 6. "We" vs "I" is a contract choice
- **I** = my view; you can disagree freely
- **We** = our view; disagreement becomes dissent

Default to "I." When writing to the -1 layer, "I think" creates more autonomy than "we should." Reserve "we" for cases where the group has genuinely signed off.

### 7. The email test
Count your non-negotiables. 0 = essay. 4+ = mandate. 1–3 = thesis.

### 8. Reader autonomy over author authority
Each section should leave room for: "I disagree," "I'd frame it differently," "I'd do it differently." If not, rewrite.

## Process

1. **Confirm genre** — proposing a view, not recommending action? Inviting disagreement, not seeking approval? Could readers overrule without you feeling undermined? Yes to all three → proceed.
2. **Draft observations first** — specific, verifiable, uncontroversial.
3. **Draft the proposition** — one paragraph per main idea.
4. **Identify non-negotiables** — cap at 3. If more, separate thesis from mandate.
5. **Identify open questions** — cap at 5. More than 5 means the thesis isn't ready.
6. **Tone pass** — "must" → "I'd want"; "will" → "would"; "we" → "I" unless genuinely collective.
7. **Reader-autonomy pass** — for each section, can a reader disagree and still engage? If no, rewrite.
8. **Mode declaration check** — the first line of the final document must declare it a thesis. Literally.

---

## Output structure

0. **Mode declaration** (one line): "This is a thesis — I'm proposing, not deciding. Tell me where I'm wrong."
1. **What I'm seeing** (observations — 2–4 bullets)
2. **What I think it means** (proposition — 1–3 paragraphs)
3. **Non-negotiables** (1–3, explicit)
4. **Open questions** (2–5, explicit)
5. **What I'd like from you** (1 ask — usually "tell me what's wrong with this")

Execution detail lives in a SEPARATE appendix document.

---

## Anti-patterns

- **Prescriptive closing**: "Based on this, we must do X." → "This points toward X. Happy to be wrong."
- **Hidden non-negotiables**: "Obviously, we'll need to…" — if it's non-negotiable, name it.
- **Binary framing**: "It's either X or Y." → "More toward X than Y."
- **No-ask closing**: Every thesis should end with one explicit ask.
- **Mode ambiguity**: Reader can't tell if it's a thesis or a mandate. Declare it at the top; don't bury the mode in tone.
